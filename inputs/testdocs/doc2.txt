CLINICAL OBSERVATIONS & BEHAVIORAL EXAMPLES
<PERSON> presented as a cooperative and engaging child. They established age-appropriate eye contact but frequently shifted in their seat, occasionally standing up to look at items on the clinician's shelf. When tasks became challenging, <PERSON> exhibited "avoidant" behaviors, such as asking for water breaks, sharpening their pencil unnecessarily, or changing the subject to discuss their favorite video games.

During the block design subtest, when they could not complete the final design within the time limit, <PERSON> pushed the blocks off the table, crossed their arms, and refused to speak for five minutes, visibly tearful.

Homework time frequently escalates into tearful breakdowns. <PERSON> will often stare at a blank worksheet for twenty minutes, paralyzed by the fear of making a mistake. When parents attempt to help, <PERSON> becomes defensive and yells, "You're confusing me on purpose!" 

Socially, <PERSON> wants to have friends but struggles with the reciprocal nature of play. They often try to dictate the rules of imaginary games and become highly distressed when peers deviate from their script, leading to frequent peer conflicts.

TEST RESULTS & DATA INTEGRATION

However, their Working Memory and Processing Speed scores fell within the Low Average range. This "bottleneck" effect explains why <PERSON> understands complex concepts but struggles to get them down on paper. The discrepancy between their intellectual capacity and their output efficiency acts as a primary catalyst for their performance anxiety.

Behavioral and Emotional Functioning
Data from the BASC-3, Conners 4, and MASC-2 indicate clinically significant elevations in several core areas:

Inattention: Both parents and teachers report that <PERSON> loses track of instructions and requires frequent redirection. Teachers note that <PERSON> often "zones out" during lectures and misses crucial transitional cues.
Emotional Reactivity: Scores suggest <PERSON> experiences "big feelings" more intensely than peers, often resulting in tearfulness or shut-down behaviors when frustrated.
Executive Functioning: Deficits were noted in planning, organizing, and initiating tasks. <PERSON> cannot organize their desk, frequently loses assignments, and struggles to estimate how long a task will take.
Anxiety/Worry: The MASC-2 revealed clinically significant elevations in Social Anxiety and Perfectionism. <PERSON> is chronically worried about peer judgment and academic failure.

DIAGNOSTIC IMPRESSION
Based on the current evaluation, <PERSON> meets the diagnostic criteria for the following:

1. Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder, Combined Presentation (F90.2)
Evidenced by a persistent pattern of inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity that interferes with functioning in two or more settings. The inattentive symptoms are primarily driving academic underperformance, while impulsivity drives social friction.

2. Generalized Anxiety Disorder (F41.1)
Characterized by excessive worry regarding academic performance, peer relationships, and social rejection, contributing to their emotional reactivity and task-avoidance behaviors.

SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS
<PERSON> is a bright and creative child whose primary challenges stem from severe executive functioning deficits colliding with generalized anxiety. To support their development, the following is highly recommended:

School-Based Supports
Individualized Education Program (IEP) or 504 Plan: To provide accommodations addressing both ADHD and Anxiety.
Extended Time & Quiet Environment: Provide 1.5x extended time on all assessments in a quiet, distraction-free room to alleviate performance anxiety.
Task Breakdown: Assignments should be broken into smaller, manageable "chunks" with frequent check-ins to prevent paralysis by overwhelming scope.
Visual Schedules & Priming: Utilize a visual timer and prime <PERSON> 5 minutes before transitions to prevent emotional dysregulation.
Scribe/Alternative Output: Allow oral responses or typed responses for lengthy writing assignments to bypass processing speed bottlenecks.

Therapeutic Interventions
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): To help <PERSON> identify and challenge "anxious thoughts" and cognitive distortions ("all-or-nothing" thinking). Therapy should focus on developing distress tolerance and coping strategies for frustration.
Parent Management Training (PMT): To assist parents in implementing consistent behavioral structures, positive reinforcement, and specialized responses to anxiety-driven defiance without escalating conflicts at home.

Medical Consultation
It is strongly recommended that the family share this report with <PERSON>’s pediatrician or a child psychiatrist to discuss the potential role of pharmacological support for ADHD symptoms and anxiety management.
